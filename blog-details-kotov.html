Kotov's Tree of Analysis: How to Calculate Without Overthinking

Daniel Carter
FIDE Senior Trainer • Tactical Specialist

Have you ever found yourself in a complex tournament position with fifteen minutes on the clock, staring at three attractive moves? You calculate Candidate Move A for four minutes, discover a small complication, jump in panic to Candidate Move B, find it dry, switch to Candidate Move C, and then—with the clock ticking down to thirty seconds—panic and return to Move A without properly verifying your first line?

This agonizing psychological spiral is what Grandmaster Alexander Kotov famously diagnosed in his seminal work Think Like a Grandmaster. The solution he proposed revolutionized chess pedagogy: the Tree of Analysis.

Step 1: The Candidate Move Census

The fatal flaw of the club player is beginning calculation immediately. When an interesting tactical shot catches your eye, resist the urge to start calculating variation moves. Instead, pause your mind and perform a candidate move census. Identify every logical forcing move: checks, captures, and threatening pawn pushes. Typically, there will be between 2 and 4 realistic candidates.

Tactical Diagram: Calculating the Decisive Sacrifice Move 19.Nxd5!

White has 3 candidate moves: 19.Nxd5!, 19.Bxh7+, and 19.Rae1. Applying Kotov's tree resolves the winning sequence in under 90 seconds.

Step 2: Calculate Each Branch Once and Only Once

Kotov's golden rule is strict mental hygiene: once you choose a branch on the tree, trace it to its logical terminus (checkmate, quiet positional equilibrium, or forced repetition). Evaluate the resulting position clearly: plus-equals, plus-minus, or unclear.

Once you have reached your conclusion, write a mental checkmark beside that branch and never calculate it again during that turn. Move immediately to Branch B. When all branches are completed, compare the static evaluations and play the best line with total confidence.

Key Rules for Rapid Tactical Clarity

  • Catalog before calculation: Never analyze move 1 until you have listed moves A, B, and C.
  • No meandering: Do not jump from branch A to branch B midway through a variation.
  • Trust your first calculation: Re-calculating lines under time pressure only injects doubt and creates phantom ghosts on the board.
"A player who calculates two moves accurately and decisively will triumph over one who dreams eight moves vaguely." — Alexander Kotov
